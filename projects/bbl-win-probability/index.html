01 / BBL win probability model — Live

A random train/test split reports 78% accuracy. The honest one reports 73%.

Ball-by-ball win probability for the Big Bash League, built from 647 matches (2011/12 – 2025/26) and tested on seasons the model never saw.

The live app, serving predictions straight from the trained model.

The question

After any delivery in a BBL match, how likely is the batting side to win? And, once a match is over, which deliveries actually decided it, measured instead of judged by eye?

What I found

1. A random split would have overstated the model by five points.

A random row-level split scores 0.781 accuracy. The chronological split scores 0.731. Those two numbers come from different rows in different seasons, so I separated the gap into its two causes. I trained two models and scored both on the same held-out rows, averaged over five random draws:

ModelAccuracyROC-AUC
A: other deliveries from the test rows' matches in training0.7790.873
B: no match in both training and test0.7550.837
Chronological, tested on 2024–260.7310.818

A vs B is leakage. Consecutive deliveries are near-duplicates, so seeing ball 71 in training makes ball 72 look familiar. That's worth 2.5 points, and A came out ahead in all five draws. B vs chronological is time. B is filling gaps inside seasons it has already seen, while the deployed model has to predict seasons after its data ends. That costs another 2.4 points. The chronological number is the one worth trusting, because predicting future seasons is the only job the model will ever do.

2. Efficiency predicts wins. Raw runs barely do.

Cumulative runs and balls faced carry 2% of the model's signal. What matters is runs per wicket, wickets lost per over, and runs measured against the target or par. Engineered features carry the other 98%.

  • Efficiency: runs per wicket, projected score33.8%
  • Context: target, required rate, runs vs par29.5%
  • Pressure: wicket-loss rate, scoring vs wicket-loss29.4%
  • Team and venue5.3%
  • Raw state: cumulative runs, balls faced2.0%

3. Setting a total and chasing one are different problems.

Chasing predictions are sharp, with a Brier score of 0.122. Predictions for the side batting first score 0.221, barely better than the 0.250 of a constant guess. This isn't a modelling failure. For the side setting a total, the biggest unknown is the innings that hasn't been bowled yet. Knowing they finished on 180 says little until the chase unfolds, and no first-innings feature can supply that.

4. A model that memorises is also overconfident.

The first version scored 0.883 accuracy on its training data and 0.727 on unseen seasons, a 15-point gap. It was also miscalibrated: when it said 83%, the side won only 64% of the time, overstated in exactly the range a coach would act on. Regularising the model cut the gap to 6 points, held test accuracy, raised AUC, and fixed the calibration in the same step. What it removed was noise, not signal.

Test results

2024–2026 matches, held out entirely. No modelling decision touched them.

Brier score 0.175 baseline 0.250, lower is better
ROC-AUC 0.818 baseline 0.500
Accuracy 0.731 baseline 0.500
Fit/test gap 0.059 down from 0.156

Brier score is the one that matters most. The model outputs a probability, not a verdict. Accuracy only checks which side of 50% a prediction landed on, so a model can be 73% accurate while claiming 85% confidence in situations that are really 60%.

How it was built

  • Data. 647 Cricsheet matches flattened to 151,331 delivery-level rows. Wides and no-balls are handled so they don't count as legal balls.
  • Chronological split. Fit on 2011 to mid-2022, validate on mid-2022 to 2023, test on 2024 onwards. That mirrors how the model is used: trained on the past, predicting matches it hasn't seen (finding 1). The validation slice drives early stopping, so the test set never influences a modelling decision.
  • Leakage prevention. The target score is withheld during the first innings. Par scores are fitted on training matches only, and the function that builds them takes training data as an argument, so the code itself enforces this.
  • Model. XGBoost with depth 4, learning rate 0.03, a minimum child weight of 20, L2 penalty 5.0, and early stopping.
  • Leverage. The change in win probability between deliveries ranks the passages that decided a match. Probabilities are flipped to a single reference team first, and the jump at the innings break is excluded and reported separately. An early version blamed that jump, a 57% swing, on a bowler who had done nothing.
  • Serving. Feature engineering lives in one module shared by training and the FastAPI prediction service, with a parity test, so the two can't drift apart.

Known limitations. The model has no player identity, so it can't tell an opener from a number nine. Its par scores are static across fifteen seasons. Its settings were reasoned through rather than tuned.
